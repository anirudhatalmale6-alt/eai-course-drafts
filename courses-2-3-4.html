Course 2 · Prompting for Reliable, Auditable Answers

Level: BeginnerLearning hours: 4Lessons: 17Price: A$590 + GST

Who it is for: anyone who has had an AI tool give them an answer that looked right and was not, and wants to stop that happening on client work.

The through line: a good prompt is not clever wording. It is a set of constraints that make a wrong answer visible.

Module 1 · What makes an answer defensible

3 lessons · 40 minutes.

1.1 Why two people get different answers from the same tool

What you will be able to do

Explain why the same question produces different answers, and stop treating that as a flaw to be worked around.

The idea

These tools predict a plausible continuation. Plausible is not the same as correct, and the same question asked twice can land on two different plausible answers. That is not a bug you can prompt your way out of. It is the reason every technique in this course exists: if the output varies, your job is to make variation detectable rather than to hope it does not happen.

Do this

Ask the same substantive question three times in three fresh chats. Not a trivia question, something with judgement in it. Put the three answers side by side and highlight where they differ. The differences are the parts you must verify on every future answer.

Takeaway

Where the answers disagree with each other is where they might disagree with the truth.

1.2 The five parts of a reliable prompt

What you will be able to do

Build any working prompt from five components, instead of writing a sentence and hoping.

The idea

Every prompt that behaves consistently has the same five parts: the role, the source, the task, the output shape, and the constraint. Miss the source and it invents. Miss the output shape and you get prose you have to re-read. Miss the constraint and it fills gaps silently.

Do this
ROLE You are reviewing this as a senior accountant. SOURCE Use only the attached trial balance and the prior year comparison. Do not use outside knowledge. TASK Identify the five accounts most likely to contain an error, and say why for each. SHAPE A table: account, movement, reason for suspicion, what I should check. LIMIT If the data does not support a suspicion, return fewer than five rows rather than filling the table.

That last line is the one people leave out, and it is the one that stops the model manufacturing a fifth answer to satisfy your request.

Check this

Remove one part and run it again. Watching the answer degrade teaches the lesson better than any explanation.

Takeaway

Role, source, task, shape, limit. Missing parts get filled in by the model, not by you.

1.3 Telling it what you do not want

What you will be able to do

Write the negative half of a prompt, which is where most of the reliability comes from.

The idea

Professionals are trained to specify what they want. With these tools, the higher-value instruction is what must not happen: do not infer, do not round, do not use outside knowledge, do not fill a gap, do not soften the finding. Each one closes off a specific failure you would otherwise have to catch by reading.

Do this

Take a prompt you already use and add three negative constraints. Run both versions on the same source and compare.

Do not infer any figure that is not stated in the document. Where something is missing, write MISSING rather than an estimate. Do not round. Do not summarise across entities.
Red line 2. Making decisions purely based on AI output without checking the underlying data, assumptions, calculations and sources. Negative constraints do not remove the need to check. They make checking possible in a reasonable amount of time.

Knowledge check, end of Module 1. Your prompt returns a tidy five-row table every time, even for a small data set. What does that most likely mean?

A. The tool is thorough
B. You asked for five rows, so it produced five rows whether or not the data supported them
C. The data set is larger than you thought

Answer: B. A requested shape is a target the model will hit. Always allow it to return less.

Module 2 · Working with long documents

3 lessons · 43 minutes. Contracts, supplier agreements, policies, tenders, reports, rulings.

2.1 Questioning a long document instead of summarising it

What you will be able to do

Get reliable answers out of a fifty-page document in minutes, without trusting a summary.

The idea

The instinct is to ask for a summary. A summary is the least useful and most dangerous output, because it flattens exactly the exceptions and carve-outs that matter and you have no way to see what it dropped. Ask narrow questions instead, and ask them one at a time.

Do this
Using only this lease, answer each question separately. For each, quote the exact clause you relied on and give the clause number. If the lease does not answer the question, say NOT ADDRESSED rather than reasoning from general knowledge. 1. What is the review mechanism and when does it apply? 2. Who pays outgoings, and are any excluded? 3. What are the make-good obligations at the end of term? 4. Is there an option to renew, and what triggers it?
Check this

Open the document at each quoted clause number. This takes two minutes and catches the failure mode that matters most: a real-sounding quote attached to the wrong clause.

The worked example below is a lease because leases punish sloppy reading. The identical structure works on a supplier agreement, an insurance policy, a tender response or a fifty-page board report. Swap the four questions for the four you would have had to find by hand.

Takeaway

Narrow questions with quoted sources. Never a summary you cannot audit.

2.2 Making it quote the clause, not paraphrase it

What you will be able to do

Force verbatim quotation, and recognise a paraphrase pretending to be one.

The idea

A paraphrase is where meaning quietly shifts. "Rent is reviewed annually to CPI" and "rent may be reviewed annually to CPI at the lessor's election" are one word apart and financially very different. Insisting on verbatim text moves the risk from interpretation, which you cannot see, to transcription, which you can check.

Do this
Quote the relevant text word for word, in quotation marks, exactly as it appears including any defined terms. Then, on a separate line, give your plain English reading. Never merge the two.
Watch out

Models will occasionally produce a fluent quotation that does not exist in the document. Search the source for a distinctive phrase from each quote. If you cannot find it, nothing else in that answer is trustworthy either.

Your input: one real de-identified contract or ruling with a genuinely tricky clause, ideally one you have seen misread before. A real trap is worth more than a clean textbook example, because the whole point of this module is catching a plausible misreading.

2.3 Comparing two versions of the same document

What you will be able to do

Find what actually changed between two drafts, including the changes nobody flagged.

The idea

Comparison is a task where these tools are genuinely strong, because it is bounded: both texts are in front of it. The discipline is asking for materiality rather than a list of every altered comma.

Do this
Compare version A and version B of this agreement. List only changes that alter an obligation, a payment, a timeframe or a risk allocation. For each: the clause, the old wording, the new wording, and who it now favours. Ignore formatting and pure drafting changes. Then separately list anything removed entirely.

"Anything removed entirely" is asked for separately because deletions are what get missed. There is no new text to notice.

Takeaway

Ask what changed materially, and always ask separately what disappeared.

Module 3 · Structured data you can trust

3 lessons · 45 minutes. Turning documents into rows.

3.1 From documents to a clean, checkable table

What you will be able to do

Extract consistent fields from a stack of inconsistent documents.

The idea

Extraction fails when the field definition is loose. "Date" is not a field. "Invoice date as printed on the document, in DD/MM/YYYY" is. Every ambiguity you leave is a decision the model makes for you, differently, on each document.

Do this
Extract these fields from each attached invoice into one table: - supplier_name: exactly as printed - abn: digits only, or MISSING - invoice_date: DD/MM/YYYY as printed, do not reformat or infer - invoice_number: as printed - subtotal_ex_gst, gst_amount, total_inc_gst: numbers only - source_file: the file name Rules: never calculate a field that is not printed. If GST is not shown, write MISSING, do not derive it. One row per invoice, no merged rows.
Check this

For every row, check that subtotal plus GST equals the total as printed. Any row that fails is a document to open by hand, and this single check catches most extraction errors in seconds.

Invoices are used here because everyone has a stack of them and the arithmetic gives you a free correctness check. The same field-by-field discipline is what you want for expense claims, timesheets, application forms, supplier onboarding packs or survey responses. The check line changes, the method does not.

Takeaway

Define the field precisely, or the model defines it for you.

3.2 Designing the output so errors are visible

What you will be able to do

Shape output so that a mistake announces itself instead of hiding.

The idea

This is the most transferable idea in the course. You cannot verify everything, so design the output so wrongness is loud. Three devices do most of the work: a MISSING marker instead of a blank, a confidence column, and a self-check the model must perform and report.

Do this
Add two columns to the table above: - confidence: high, medium or low, based on how clearly the field was printed - flag: any row where a field was hard to read, where two values could have been the field, or where the arithmetic does not tie Then, below the table, state: how many rows you produced, how many documents you were given, and any document you could not process at all.

Row count against document count is a ten-second check that catches silently skipped files, which is the extraction error people notice last.

Takeaway

Make the output tell you where to look.

3.3 Spot-checking a batch without checking all of it

What you will be able to do

Verify a two hundred row extraction in about ten minutes, defensibly.

The idea

Checking everything defeats the purpose; checking nothing is negligent. The middle path is a deliberate sample: everything flagged, everything low confidence, the largest few by value, and a small random handful. That is a sampling approach your profession already understands, applied to a new source of error.

Do this

Take a real extraction and check: all flagged rows, all low-confidence rows, the five largest by value, and five chosen at random. Record what you checked and what you found. That record is the file note.

Red line 4. Situations where there is no clear audit trail or where you cannot explain how the conclusion was reached. "We extracted 200 invoices with AI" is not a method. "We extracted 200 invoices, checked all 14 flagged rows plus 10 others by value and at random, and found 2 errors which we corrected" is.

Knowledge check, end of Module 3. Your extraction returns 198 rows from 200 documents and everything ties. What do you do?

A. Proceed, a 99 percent rate is excellent
B. Find the two missing documents, because a skipped file is invisible in the output
C. Re-run the whole batch

Answer: B. Errors you can see are the manageable kind. Absences are the dangerous kind.

Module 4 · Writing that goes out under your name

3 lessons · 40 minutes. Client advice, internal recommendations, board and management updates.

4.1 Drafting so the reviewer edits rather than rewrites

What you will be able to do

Produce a first draft, of advice or a recommendation, that a reviewer improves rather than rewrites.

The idea

The reason AI-drafted work gets rewritten is that it answers the question in general rather than for this reader and this situation. Give it the client's specific facts, the conclusion you have already reached, and the reasoning you want shown. You are asking it to write up your thinking, not to do your thinking.

Do this
Write a client letter setting out the following advice. The conclusion is mine and is not open for revision: [your conclusion] The facts you may rely on are these and only these: [facts] Structure: what we were asked, what we found, what we recommend, what we need from you. Do not add caveats I have not given you. Do not restate the law in general terms. If a fact is needed that I have not provided, mark it [FACT NEEDED] rather than assuming it.
Check this

Read the draft asking one question only: does it say anything I did not decide? Anything it added is either a fact it invented or a caveat that changes your advice.

Red line 1. Final decisions that require professional judgement, accountability or sign-off. The conclusion is yours before the drafting starts. If you find yourself reading the draft to discover what you think, stop.

4.2 Matching your voice, not the model's

What you will be able to do

Get output that sounds like your firm, reliably, without editing every sentence.

The idea

Describing a tone does not work. "Professional but warm" produces the same generic register for everyone. Showing examples works. Two or three pieces of your own real writing will do more than a paragraph of adjectives.

Do this
Here are three letters we have sent. Study the sentence length, how we open, how we deliver bad news, and how much we explain before we recommend. Match those patterns. Do not match the content. Now draft the following letter in that voice: [brief]
Your input: two or three real client letters, de-identified, that you would be happy to hold up as house style. These become a downloadable asset for learners to use as their own template, and they are the difference between this lesson being useful and being theory.
Takeaway

Show it your writing. Do not describe your writing.

4.3 The hedging problem, and how to remove it

What you will be able to do

Strip out the padding that makes AI writing recognisable and slightly evasive.

The idea

These tools hedge by default: it is important to note, this may vary depending on circumstances, we recommend consulting a professional. In client advice this is worse than clutter. It reads as though you are not sure, and it dilutes the one recommendation you are being paid for.

Do this
Rewrite the draft below. Remove every sentence that does not carry information. Delete phrases like "it is important to note", "this may vary", and any recommendation to seek professional advice, since we are the professional advice. Keep every number and every deadline. Aim for about 30 percent shorter without losing content.
Check this

Compare the two versions and confirm nothing substantive was cut alongside the padding. Aggressive shortening occasionally takes a real qualifier with it.

Takeaway

Hedging is not caution. It is the absence of a decision.

Module 5 · Research and citation

2 lessons · 29 minutes. The module that prevents the most embarrassing failure.

5.1 Making the model show its sources

What you will be able to do

Get research output where every claim is traceable, and know which tools can honestly do this.

The idea

There is a real difference between a tool that searches and cites what it found, and one that answers from memory and then produces a citation that looks right. The second is where fabricated references come from. Knowing which mode you are in is more important than any prompt.

Do this
Answer using only sources you have actually retrieved in this session. For each claim, give the source and a direct quote from it. If you did not retrieve a source for a claim, mark it UNSOURCED. Do not produce a citation you cannot quote from.
Check this

Open every citation. Not a sample, every one. Fabricated references are plausible enough to survive a glance and catastrophic in professional work.

The Australian frame. A citation to an ATO ruling or an accounting standard that does not exist, or that says something different, is the kind of error that ends up in a professional complaint. This lesson exists because the failure is quiet and the consequence is not.

5.2 Recognising a confident answer built on nothing

What you will be able to do

Notice, from the answer alone, when the model is guessing.

The idea

There are tells. Round numbers with no source. Generalities where you asked for specifics. Confident dates. A reference to "recent guidance" without naming it. Once you have seen the pattern a few times you will feel it before you can articulate it, and the fix is the same every time: ask it to show you.

Do this

Deliberately ask a question you know the tool cannot answer from your material, and study how it responds. Learning the shape of a guess on a low-stakes question is much cheaper than learning it on a client matter.

For your previous answer, mark each sentence as: stated in the source, inferred from the source, or from general knowledge. Be strict with yourself.
Takeaway

Fluency is not evidence. Ask it to mark its own working.

Module 6 · Reviewing, not just producing

2 lessons · 28 minutes. The underused half of the tool.

6.1 Using AI to review a spreadsheet or a draft

What you will be able to do

Get a genuine second pair of eyes on work you or a colleague produced.

The idea

Most people use these tools to produce and then check the output themselves. The inverse is often more valuable: you produce, and it reviews. It is tireless, it has no ego about your model, and it does not get bored on row 400.

Do this
Review this workbook as a careful reviewer would. Look for: formulas inconsistent with the rest of their row or column, hard-coded numbers inside formulas, references pointing outside the intended range, totals that do not equal the sum of their parts, and anything labelled in a way that does not match what it calculates. List findings by severity with the cell reference. Do not change anything.
Check this

Verify each finding before acting. Review output has false positives, and a confident finding about a formula that is actually fine wastes time if you accept it blindly.

Takeaway

You produce, it reviews. Often more valuable than the other way round.

6.2 The adversarial prompt: asking it to argue against you

What you will be able to do

Stress-test your own conclusion before someone else does.

The idea

These tools are agreeable by default, which makes them poor critics unless instructed otherwise. Told explicitly to argue the other side, they become genuinely useful, because they have no stake in your being right.

Do this
Here is my conclusion and the reasoning behind it. Argue against it as strongly as the evidence allows. Identify the weakest link, what assumption it rests on, and what evidence would overturn it. Do not be balanced. Do not reassure me.
Takeaway

Ask it to attack your work before your client, your partner or the board does.

Module 7 · Your prompt library

1 lesson · 14 minutes. Turning the course into something the team keeps.

7.1 Building and sharing a prompt library that gets used

What you will be able to do

Leave with a small library of prompts your team will actually reuse.

The idea

Most prompt libraries die because they are too big and stored somewhere nobody looks. Ten prompts in a shared document beats a hundred in a system nobody opens. Each entry needs four things: what it is for, the prompt, what to check afterwards, and who owns it.

Do this

Take the five prompts from this course you would actually use, adapt them to your firm's wording, and put them where your team already works. Add the check line to each. Without the check line it is just a shortcut, and shortcuts are how the habits you have just built get lost.

Your input: where your target firms actually keep shared resources. SharePoint, Teams, a wiki, or realistically a Word document on a shared drive. I will write the lesson for wherever they really are rather than where they should be.
Takeaway

A prompt without its check line is half a prompt.

Practical task and final quiz

Practical task: take a real document you are working on this week and answer three questions from it using the Module 2 method, with quoted clauses. Submit the questions, the quotes, and what you found when you checked them.

Final quiz sample. A colleague sends you a research note drafted with AI, citing three ATO rulings. What do you do first?

A. Check the reasoning is sound
B. Open all three rulings and confirm they exist and say what is claimed
C. Rewrite it in the firm's voice

Answer: B. Sound reasoning built on a citation that does not exist is still worthless, and it is the failure most likely to reach a client.

Your input for Course 2: (1) one real de-identified contract or ruling with a tricky clause; (2) two or three house-style client letters; (3) where firms keep shared resources. Everything else is drafted.

Course 3 · Automating Business & Finance Workflows with AI Agents

Level: IntermediateLearning hours: 5Lessons: 18Price: A$590 + GST

Who it is for: someone who has used AI for individual tasks and now wants a process to run without them standing over it.

The through line: the course builds one real agent, start to finish, and the hardest lessons are about what you deliberately do not automate.

Structural note: this course is a build. The learner picks a process in Module 2 and carries it through to production in Module 7, so what they finish with is a working thing rather than a set of notes.

Module 1 · What an agent is, and when it beats a prompt

3 lessons · 42 minutes.

1.1 Prompt, automation or agent: telling them apart

What you will be able to do

Choose the right level of tool for a task, and stop reaching for the most complex one.

The idea

A prompt is you asking, once. An automation follows fixed rules with no judgement. An agent is given a goal and decides its own steps. Most finance problems people describe as needing an agent are actually automations, and a rules-based automation you can read is nearly always better than an agent you cannot predict.

Do this

List five tasks you would like off your plate. For each, ask whether the steps are the same every time. If yes, it is an automation, and you should build that instead. Only the ones where the steps genuinely vary belong in this course.

Takeaway

Use the least clever tool that does the job. Cleverness is unpredictability.

1.2 What agents are genuinely good at, and what they are not

What you will be able to do

Recognise the shape of a task where an agent earns its complexity.

The idea

Agents shine where inputs arrive in unpredictable formats but the intent is consistent. Supplier invoices from forty suppliers in forty layouts, all needing the same six fields. Emails that might be a query, an approval or a complaint. The variation is in the input, not in what you want done.

Do this

For each candidate from 1.1, write down what varies: the input format, the decision, or the output. If only the input format varies, it is a good agent candidate. If the decision varies, be careful. If the output varies, you probably have several processes wearing one name.

Takeaway

Varying inputs, consistent intent. That is the sweet spot.

1.3 The processes you should refuse to automate

What you will be able to do

Say no to an automation request, with reasons that hold up.

The idea

This lesson is the reason the course is credible. There are processes where full automation is the wrong answer regardless of how well it would work technically, and being able to explain why is a senior skill. Your five limits give the test.

Do this

Run each candidate through five questions. Does it end in a decision requiring sign-off? Could it act on data nobody checked? Does it touch sensitive information in a tool without controls? Would the trail show how the conclusion was reached? Is it a compliance, tax, audit, credit or investment decision?

Red line 5. Fully automating high-risk decisions involving compliance, tax, audit, credit, investment or regulatory obligations without appropriate human oversight. Note the words "fully" and "without oversight". The answer is rarely do not automate. It is automate the preparation, keep the decision.

Knowledge check, end of Module 1. A partner asks for an agent that reviews expense claims and approves anything under 500 dollars. What is the right response?

A. Build it, the threshold makes it low risk
B. Build it to prepare and recommend, with a person approving in one click, and log both
C. Refuse, approvals cannot be automated

Answer: B. A one-click human approval keeps accountability where it belongs and costs almost nothing in time. C loses all the value; A moves the accountability to nobody.

Module 2 · Choosing the right process

3 lessons · 46 minutes. The learner picks the process they will build.

2.1 Scoring a candidate process: volume, rules, risk

What you will be able to do

Rank your candidate processes objectively instead of by whichever annoys you most.

The idea

Three scores decide it. Volume: how often does it run, and how long does it take. Rule clarity: could you write the rules down for a new starter. Risk: what happens if it goes wrong and nobody notices. High volume plus clear rules plus low risk is where you start. The most irritating task is usually high risk, which is exactly why it is still manual.

Do this

Score each candidate out of five on volume, rule clarity and inverse risk. Build the highest total first. Keep the scoring sheet; it is what you show your manager to justify the effort.

Takeaway

Start where the volume is high and the consequences are low. Earn trust before you spend it.

2.2 Mapping the process as it actually runs today

What you will be able to do

Document the real process, including the parts nobody admits to.

The idea

Every process has an official version and a real one. The real one has the exceptions, the person who is emailed when something looks odd, and the spreadsheet somebody keeps on the side. Automating the official version produces something that breaks in week one.

Do this

Sit with whoever runs it and walk through the last ten real instances, not a description of the process. Ask what they did each time. Write down every exception. Then ask the question that finds the rest: "when does this go wrong, and what do you do then?"

Your input: one real process from a client, de-identified, with its genuine exceptions. The exceptions are the whole lesson. A clean process map teaches nothing because clean processes do not need this course.
Takeaway

Map what happens, not what is supposed to happen.

2.3 Deciding where the human sign-off sits

What you will be able to do

Place the approval step deliberately, before you build anything.

The idea

Sign-off placed at the end is usually theatre, because by then the work is done and approving is the path of least resistance. Placed at the point where the decision is actually made, it is real. Deciding this before you build means the agent is designed around the checkpoint rather than having one bolted on.

Do this

On your process map, mark the single point where a wrong step becomes expensive to undo. The sign-off goes immediately before it, not at the end.

Takeaway

Put the approval where the decision is, not where the process ends.

Module 3 · Your first agent

3 lessons · 52 minutes. Built with Power Automate and Copilot, which your firm already has.

3.1 Building an agent with what you already have

What you will be able to do

Assemble a working agent from Power Automate and Copilot, without buying anything.

The idea

An agent is not a product you buy, it is four parts wired together: something that triggers it, something that reads and reasons, somewhere the rules live, and somewhere the trail is written. Copilot Studio bundles those four into one screen. Power Automate makes you assemble them yourself, which is slower to build and considerably better to learn on, because you can see every join. Nothing is hidden inside a product you are renting.

This matters beyond convenience. When a partner asks how the thing decided something, you can point at the step. That is much harder to do with a tool that presents itself as a single box.

Do this

Build a flow triggered by a file landing in a SharePoint or Teams folder. Have it pass the document to Copilot with a fixed instruction, write the result to a spreadsheet, and record what it did. Use a document you know well and check every field by hand the first time.

Watch out

Power Automate will happily run on a schedule forever with nobody reading the output. Build the log before you build the automation, not after.

Takeaway

You do not need a licence you do not have. You need a trigger, a reasoning step, written rules and a trail.

If your firm does have Copilot Studio. Everything in this module transfers directly. The four parts are the same parts, just assembled for you on one screen. Lesson 3.3 closes with what changes and when the upgrade is worth it, so nobody who has paid for it is left out.

3.2 Building a working agent from your process map

What you will be able to do

Turn the map from Module 2 into an agent that handles the normal case end to end.

The idea

Build the happy path first and resist the urge to handle everything. An agent that manages the seventy percent of cases that are straightforward, and hands the rest to a person, delivers most of the value at a fraction of the complexity.

Do this

Write the agent's instructions as you would brief a capable new starter: what it is for, what it must always do, what it must never do, and when to stop and ask. Then connect the sources it needs.

You process supplier invoices for approval. ALWAYS: extract supplier, ABN, date, number, amounts and GST exactly as printed. Match to a purchase order where one exists. NEVER: approve a payment. Never calculate GST that is not printed. Never guess a supplier from a partial name. STOP AND ASK when: no PO match, the amount differs from the PO by more than 5 percent, the supplier is new, or anything is unreadable. ALWAYS RECORD: what you extracted, what you matched it to, and why you stopped if you stopped.
Takeaway

Brief it like a new starter. Always, never, stop and ask, record.

3.3 Testing it against the messy cases first

What you will be able to do

Test in the order that finds problems, rather than the order that feels reassuring.

The idea

Everyone tests the clean case, watches it work and feels good. Test the mess first: the scanned document, the credit note, the foreign currency invoice, the duplicate, the one with two pages stapled the wrong way round. If it handles those or stops sensibly, the clean case is not in doubt.

Do this

Assemble ten genuinely awkward real examples before you test a single normal one. For each, record what you expected, what happened, and whether stopping would have been the right answer.

Watch out

The dangerous outcome is not an error. It is the agent confidently processing something it should have stopped on. Count those separately, because they are the ones that reach production.

Takeaway

Mess first. A confident wrong answer is worse than a stop.

Module 4 · Document intake

3 lessons · 48 minutes. The most common real use case.

4.1 From email attachment to a processed record

What you will be able to do

Build the full chain: an invoice arrives by email, and a coded draft entry is waiting for approval.

The idea

This is the flagship example because everyone has it and everyone hates it. The chain is: receive, extract, match, code, queue for approval. Each link can fail differently, so each gets its own check rather than one check at the end.

Do this

Build the chain one link at a time, confirming each before adding the next. Coding is where firm knowledge lives, so give it your actual chart of accounts and your real coding rules, including the ones that are conventions rather than rules.

Check this

Run twenty real invoices through and compare the agent's coding with what your team actually did. Disagreements are either agent errors or undocumented conventions, and both are worth finding.

Your input: a real, anonymised chart of accounts and two or three coding rules that are genuinely judgement calls, the kind a new starter gets wrong. That is what makes this lesson expert rather than generic.

4.2 Handling the documents that do not fit the pattern

What you will be able to do

Design the exception path properly, so odd documents are handled rather than lost.

The idea

Exceptions are not failures, they are a category. Credit notes, statements sent instead of invoices, multi-page bundles, foreign currency, prepayments spanning periods. Each needs a named destination. An exception with no destination becomes an item nobody owns.

Do this

List every exception type from your testing and give each a route: to a person, to a queue, or to a different process. Then check that every route has an owner and a review interval.

Takeaway

Every exception needs a name and an owner, or it becomes a backlog.

4.3 The approval step, and what it must show

What you will be able to do

Build an approval screen that lets someone approve responsibly in seconds.

The idea

An approval step that shows only a total trains people to click yes. It must show the source document alongside the extracted values, what it matched to, what it changed, and anything it was unsure about. Enough to actually check, little enough to be quick.

Do this

Design the approval view, then hand it to a colleague with three invoices, one of which has a deliberate error. If they catch it, the view is good. If they do not, it is showing the wrong things.

Red line 4. Situations where there is no clear audit trail or where you cannot explain how the conclusion was reached. If someone asks in a year why an invoice was coded that way, the answer must be on the record, not in the agent's head.

Module 5 · Exception monitoring

2 lessons · 28 minutes.

5.1 Watching for the things people forget to check

What you will be able to do

Put an agent on the checks that only get done when someone remembers.

The idea

Monitoring is the safest possible use of an agent, because it only ever tells a person something. Duplicate payments, a supplier's bank details changing, a debtor crossing terms, an unusual journal at an unusual hour. None of these are hard to detect. They are just easy to forget.

Do this

Pick three checks nobody currently does reliably. Define exactly what triggers each, who is told, and what they should do about it. If the last answer is unclear, the check is not ready.

Your input: the three checks you most often find missing when you look at a client's controls. Real ones from real engagements will be far more persuasive than my generic list.

5.2 Alerts that get acted on rather than muted

What you will be able to do

Design alerts people keep reading after the second week.

The idea

An alert nobody acts on is worse than none, because it creates the appearance of a control. The rules are simple and widely ignored: alert on the exception rather than the event, include enough context to decide without opening anything else, and state the action. If you cannot say what the person should do, do not send it.

Do this

Write your three alerts as they will actually appear, then review them a week later and count how many prompted an action. Anything below half needs its threshold changed or should be deleted.

Takeaway

If you cannot name the action, do not send the alert.

Module 6 · Human in the loop

2 lessons · 31 minutes. The module that makes the rest defensible.

6.1 Designing a sign-off that is real, not a rubber stamp

What you will be able to do

Tell the difference between oversight and its appearance, in your own build.

The idea

Real sign-off has three properties: the person can see what they are approving, they have a genuine ability to reject, and rejection has somewhere to go. Remove any one and it becomes a rubber stamp. The tell is the approval rate. If nothing is ever rejected, nobody is really reviewing.

Do this

Look at your approval step and ask what a person would have to do to reject something, and what happens then. If rejecting is harder than approving, people will approve.

Red line 1. Final decisions that require professional judgement, accountability or sign-off. Automation does not move accountability. It only changes how much information the accountable person has when they sign.

6.2 What the agent must record about every decision

What you will be able to do

Specify the log so a year-old decision can be reconstructed.

The idea

Six fields cover almost everything: what came in, what the agent extracted or decided, what it matched against, what it was unsure about, who approved and when, and what changed after approval. Written down at design time, this costs nothing. Retrofitted after an incident, it is impossible.

Do this

Write the six fields for your agent and confirm each is actually captured. Then pick a processed item at random and reconstruct it from the log alone, without opening anything else.

Takeaway

If you cannot rebuild the decision from the log, you do not have a log.

Module 7 · Running it in production

2 lessons · 29 minutes. The part every course skips.

7.1 When it breaks: failure modes and safe stopping

What you will be able to do

Make the agent fail safely rather than quietly.

The idea

There are three failure modes. It stops, which is fine and visible. It errors, which is fine if someone is told. Or it keeps going while being wrong, which is the one that costs money. Design so the third becomes the second: volume limits, sanity checks on its own output, and a hard stop when the rate of unusual cases rises.

Do this

Add three guards: a maximum number of items per run, a check that its own output is within expected ranges, and an automatic stop if exceptions exceed a threshold. Then deliberately break it and confirm you found out.

Takeaway

Make it stop loudly. Silence is the expensive failure.

7.2 Handover: making it survive you leaving

What you will be able to do

Document the agent so someone else can own it.

The idea

The most common end for an internal automation is that the person who built it moves on and nobody dares touch it. One page prevents this: what it does, what it must never do, who owns it, what to check monthly, how to turn it off, and what breaks if the input format changes.

Do this

Write that page. Then have a colleague turn the agent off and on using only your page. If they cannot, it is not finished.

Takeaway

An agent nobody but you can turn off is a risk, not an asset.

Practical task and final quiz

Practical task: the agent itself. The learner submits their process map, the agent's instructions, their exception routes and their handover page. This is the capstone of the course and the most substantial artefact in the whole program.

Final quiz sample. Your invoice agent has run for a month with a 100 percent approval rate. What does that tell you?

A. It is working perfectly
B. The approval step may not be functioning as a control, and should be investigated
C. The threshold is set too low

Answer: B. It might be perfect. But an approval that never rejects anything is indistinguishable from no approval, and you cannot tell which you have without looking.

Module 1 · What your obligations already say

3 lessons · 45 minutes.

1.1 You are already regulated: what applies without new AI law

What you will be able to do

Explain, without waiting for AI-specific legislation, what already governs your firm's use of these tools.

The idea

The most common governance mistake is waiting for a rule that names AI. Your existing obligations already cover it: confidentiality, competence, supervision, record keeping, privacy, and the duty to exercise professional judgement. None of them mention AI, and all of them apply to it. A tool that helps you breach an existing obligation is a problem today, not when a new act commences.

Do this

List your firm's five most significant existing obligations. For each, write one sentence on how an AI tool could cause a breach. That list is the foundation for everything else in the course, and it is usually the moment the topic stops feeling abstract.

Answered, 17 August. The audience is CPAs and CAs, both in-house finance teams and accounting or tax practices. Financial planners are explicitly out of scope, which removes a whole separate regime and keeps this module sharp.

Three standards carry the course: APES 110 for confidentiality and professional competence, the Code of Professional Conduct under the Tax Agent Services Act for taking reasonable care, and the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles, particularly cross-border disclosure. APES 110 Part 2 covers members in business and Part 3 members in public practice, so one code carries both halves of the audience and this module does not need splitting. What differs is whose data it is, a client's or an employer's, which is taught once and landed twice.
Takeaway

Nothing is waiting for AI law. Your obligations already arrived.

1.2 Australia's National AI Plan, and what it means for you

What you will be able to do

Explain the national direction to your board or your team in two minutes, and say what it means practically.

The idea

The plan's posture is adoption with appropriate safeguards, rather than restriction. That matters for a firm in two ways. It signals that a blanket ban is out of step with the national direction, and it puts the emphasis on being able to show how you are using AI responsibly. The practical translation is simple: you need a position, and you need evidence for it.

Do this

Write your firm's one-paragraph position on AI, as you would say it to a client who asked. Not a policy yet, just a position. Most firms discover they do not have one, and that is the useful finding.

Why this matters commercially. Clients are starting to ask their accountants and advisers whether AI touched their work. A firm with a clear position answers confidently, and it becomes a mark of quality. A firm without one either overclaims or goes quiet, and both damage trust.
Decided, 17 August. Light touch, and for a better reason than I first gave. The National AI Plan is short, and its position is that Australia will not introduce mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI, on the basis that existing law already covers it.

That is not a footnote, it is the spine of this course. There is no AI statute coming to tell an accountant what to do. The obligations that bite are the ones they already carry: APES 110, the tax agent code, the Privacy Act. So the plan gets referenced twice, as national direction, and the lesson teaches the existing duties rather than waiting for new ones. That framing also ages well, which quoting the plan would not.

1.3 Where the duty of care actually bites

What you will be able to do

Identify the specific moments in your firm's work where AI use could become a professional problem.

The idea

Duty of care is not breached by using a tool. It is breached at identifiable moments: relying on an output you did not check, sending work you cannot explain, putting confidential information somewhere you should not, or letting a junior use a tool without supervision because nobody said not to. Those are the four moments, and each has a control.

Do this

Walk one real engagement end to end and mark every point where an AI tool could be used. For each, name who would use it, on what data, and who would check. The gaps in that table are your real risks, and they are usually not where people expect.

Red line 1. Final decisions that require professional judgement, accountability or sign-off, especially where the consequences are material. This is the sentence the rest of the course implements.

Knowledge check, end of Module 1. Your firm has no AI policy and a junior uses a public chatbot to summarise a client file. Whose problem is it?

A. The junior's, they should have known
B. The firm's, because supervision and confidentiality obligations sit with the firm regardless of policy
C. Nobody's, there was no rule

Answer: B. The absence of a policy is itself the finding. "We had no rule" is not a defence, it is an aggravating fact.

Module 2 · The board conversation

3 lessons · 44 minutes. The most immediately useful module for a senior buyer.

2.1 The five questions a board will ask about AI

What you will be able to do

Walk into a board meeting knowing precisely what you will be asked.

The idea

Boards ask the same five questions, in roughly this order. Are we using it? Where is our client data going? What could go wrong and how would we know? Are we behind our competitors? Who is accountable? Every one has a good answer and a bad answer, and the bad answers are all versions of "we are looking into it".

Do this

Write your current honest answer to each of the five. Then mark which you could evidence today. The gap between what you would say and what you could show is the work programme for the rest of this course.

Takeaway

Five questions. Know them before the meeting, not during it.

2.2 Answering them with evidence rather than reassurance

What you will be able to do

Replace confident-sounding answers with short, checkable ones.

The idea

"We take data security very seriously" is a non-answer, and experienced directors hear it as one. "Client data stays in our Microsoft tenant, three tools are approved, two are prohibited, and here is the one-page matrix" is an answer. The difference is not confidence. It is whether there is an artefact behind the sentence.

Do this

For each of the five questions, name the single artefact that supports your answer: the approved-tool matrix, the policy, the incident log, the training record. If a question has no artefact, it currently has no answer.

Your input: the real questions you have been asked by boards or partners, in their words. Anything from your advisory conversations would make this module unmistakably first-hand rather than reconstructed, which is exactly what a senior buyer can tell the difference between.

2.3 What to put in the board paper, and what to leave out

What you will be able to do

Write a one-page AI update for a board that gets read and does not create work.

The idea

Boards want position, exposure, and decision required. They do not want a technology explainer, and including one signals you have misjudged the audience. One page: where we are, what we have controlled, what we have not yet, and what we want the board to decide or note.

Do this

Draft that page for your own firm. Then cut it by a third. Whatever survives is the part that mattered.

Takeaway

Position, exposure, decision. Never an explainer.

Module 3 · Tools and data

3 lessons · 45 minutes. Where governance becomes concrete.

3.1 Classifying your data before you approve any tool

What you will be able to do

Sort your firm's information into three or four tiers your team can apply without thinking hard.

The idea

Tool approval is impossible without data classification, because the question is never "is this tool safe" but "is this tool appropriate for this information". Three tiers are usually enough: public, internal, and client confidential, with personal or sensitive information called out separately because it carries its own obligations.

Do this

Take ten real documents from this week and assign each a tier. Where you hesitate, the definitions need sharpening, and that hesitation is more valuable than a clean first pass.

Takeaway

Classify the data first. Tool decisions follow from it.

3.2 Approving tools: the questions to ask a vendor

What you will be able to do

Assess an AI vendor without needing to be technical.

The idea

Six questions get you most of the way. Where is our data stored and processed. Is it used to train their models, and can we turn that off. Who at the vendor can access it. How long is it retained and can we delete it. What happens to our data if we leave. Can you tell us if there is a breach, and how quickly. A vendor who cannot answer these plainly has told you something.

Do this

Send the six questions to a tool your firm already uses. The answers, or the silence, are the lesson.

Your input, now narrower: anything you would add from your own vendor assessments. The framing question is settled: I will map these against the government's Guidance for AI Adoption and its six essential practices, which superseded the ten voluntary guardrails in October 2025. That is the most current Australian reference and it is the one a client's own advisers will recognise. I will confirm the wording is still current before it goes into the lesson rather than working from memory.

3.3 The approved-use matrix your team can actually follow

What you will be able to do

Produce a single page that tells everyone which tool may be used on which data.

The idea

This is the artefact that does the most work in the whole course. Tools down the side, data tiers across the top, and a yes, no or with-conditions in each cell. It answers the day-to-day question directly, which a policy document does not, and it fits on a wall.

Do this

Build the matrix for your firm, then test it: ask three colleagues what they would do in three real situations. If they get different answers, a cell is ambiguous.

Red line 3. Handling highly sensitive or confidential financial information in public AI tools without appropriate data controls. The matrix is how that sentence becomes something a team can follow on a Tuesday afternoon.

Module 4 · Assessing a use case

2 lessons · 29 minutes.

4.1 A one-page risk assessment that takes 20 minutes

What you will be able to do

Assess a proposed AI use before it starts, quickly enough that people actually do it.

The idea

A heavy assessment process gets bypassed, and bypassed governance is worse than none because it is invisible. One page, twenty minutes, six questions: what is the use, whose data, what could go wrong, who would notice, who signs off, and how would we stop it. Anything longer will be skipped by the people you most need to reach.

Do this

Run the assessment on something your firm is already doing with AI, not a hypothetical. Assessing an existing use is more uncomfortable and considerably more useful.

Takeaway

Governance people skip is worse than none. Keep it to one page.

4.2 Deciding to proceed, proceed with controls, or decline

What you will be able to do

Make and record a decision that will still look reasonable in two years.

The idea

There are only three outcomes, and the middle one is where most cases land. Proceed with controls means naming the controls and who owns them, not writing "with appropriate safeguards". Decline needs a reason and a review date, because the answer may change as the tools do.

Do this

Record your decision on the use case from 4.1 in three lines: the decision, the reasons, the review date. That is the whole record, and it is the thing a regulator or an insurer will ask to see.

Red line 5. Fully automating high-risk decisions involving compliance, tax, audit, credit, investment or regulatory obligations without appropriate human oversight. In assessment terms this is rarely a decline. It is proceed with controls, where the control is a named human.

Module 5 · The policy

2 lessons · 29 minutes. The capstone artefact.

5.1 The one-page AI policy every firm needs

What you will be able to do

Write a policy your team can read in three minutes and actually follow.

The idea

Long policies are written to protect the firm and read by nobody. One page, six sections: what we believe, what is approved, what is prohibited, what always requires a human, what to do if something goes wrong, and who to ask. Prohibitions come with reasons, because a rule people understand survives contact with a deadline.

Do this

Draft it from the template. The prohibited section writes itself from your five limits, which is why they were taught first.

5.2 Making the policy something people follow

What you will be able to do

Get a policy adopted rather than filed.

The idea

Adoption is a separate problem from drafting and it is the one firms lose. Four things move it: someone senior says it out loud, it is short enough to read, it says yes to something rather than only no, and there is an obvious place to ask a question. A policy that only prohibits drives usage underground, which is the outcome you were trying to prevent.

Do this

Name the person who will introduce it, the meeting where it will be introduced, and the channel for questions. Put a date on all three. Without those, it is a document rather than a change.

Takeaway

A policy that only says no gets ignored quietly. Say yes to something.

Module 6 · When it goes wrong

2 lessons · 29 minutes.

6.1 Incident response: the first hour

What you will be able to do

Know what to do in the first hour after discovering an AI-related problem.

The idea

Incidents here have a particular shape. Confidential information was put somewhere it should not have been. An output went to a client and was wrong. A tool was used against policy. The first hour is the same in each case: contain, establish the facts, decide who needs to know, and write it down as you go rather than afterwards.

Do this

Run a tabletop exercise. "A junior pasted a client's financials into a public chatbot last Tuesday and has just told you." Work through the hour with your team. It takes twenty minutes and changes how seriously the policy is taken.

Your input: two or three realistic scenarios based on what you have actually seen or heard of. Real scenarios land differently from invented ones, and this is the lesson people will remember and repeat.

6.2 Telling a client or your board something went wrong

What you will be able to do

Have the conversation you are dreading, well.

The idea

The instinct is to minimise, and it is always the wrong call. What preserves a relationship is speed, specificity and what you have already changed. Clients forgive an error handled openly. They rarely forgive discovering it themselves, and they never forgive being managed.

Do this

Draft the notification for the scenario from 6.1. Four parts: what happened, what it means for you, what we have done, what we are changing. No adjectives, no self-defence.

Takeaway

Fast, specific and already fixed. In that order.
