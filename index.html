1. The framework, in one idea

Every course teaches a real finance, accounting or tax task, then has the learner do that task with AI, safely. We are not teaching AI theory. We are teaching a professional to finish real work faster and defend the result. That single idea is our difference from a generic AI course, and it is what an OpenAI Select Partner should sound like.

I looked at how the two academies you named are built, so ours borrows what works and adds what they do not have.

What OpenAI Academy does

Free and self paced. Built around real workplace tasks and hands on activities. A clear pathway (foundations, then applied, then agents and workflows). Short courses of roughly 60 to 90 minutes. Completion certificate.

What Anthropic Academy does

Video lessons, hands on exercises, a knowledge check along the way, and a quiz at the end of each course. Organised into tracks. An official certificate you add to LinkedIn.

What we add on top

A finance, accounting and tax lens on every example. Source traceability and professional judgement built into each lesson. An Australian regulatory frame. And a certificate people are proud to share, which we already built, with one click Add to LinkedIn.

Six design principles we reuse everywhere

  1. Task first, not tool first. Each lesson starts from a job the learner already does (a reconciliation, an email to a client, a board pack), then shows the AI way.
  2. Short lessons. 8 to 16 minutes each. A professional learns in the gaps of a working day, so nothing is longer than one coffee.
  3. Show, then do. Every concept is followed by a worked example and a short hands on exercise using a real template.
  4. Always defensible. Every lesson reinforces checking the output, keeping a source trail, and applying professional judgement. This is our brand.
  5. Reusable assets. Prompts, checklists and policy templates are built once and shared across courses, and learners download them to keep.
  6. Prove it. Knowledge checks during the course and a short quiz at the end, then the certificate. Learning that is measured, not just watched.

2. The lesson anatomy

This is the repeatable shape of every lesson. It keeps the courses consistent, makes them fast to produce, and gives you an obvious place to add your expertise (steps 4 and 7).

  1. Why this matters. One or two lines on the real cost of doing it the old way.
  2. What you will be able to do. A single, concrete learning outcome.
  3. The idea, in plain English. The concept, no jargon.
  4. In your firm. A concrete finance, accounting or tax example. (Your input makes these real.)
  5. Show me. A short walkthrough: the exact steps, or a prompt and its output, with a screenshot.
  6. Try it. A hands on exercise with a supplied template, so the learner does it once themselves.
  7. Watch out. The common mistake, the risk, or the governance note. (Your professional judgement lives here.)
  8. Takeaway. One sentence they will remember.

3. Quizzes and how we prove learning

Two layers, matching what the OpenAI and Anthropic academies do, kept light so professionals are not put off.

Knowledge checks (during the course)

2 to 3 questions at the end of each module. Low stakes, instant feedback, they can retry. The point is to make a concept stick, not to grade.

Final quiz (to earn the certificate)

8 to 10 questions at the end of the course. Pass mark 80 percent, unlimited attempts. Passing unlocks the certificate and the Add to LinkedIn button we already built.

Question styles we use

  • Scenario. "A client emails asking X. Which Copilot approach keeps the answer defensible?" This tests judgement, not memory, which suits your audience.
  • Best next step. Given a half finished task, what would you do next.
  • Spot the risk. Show an AI output and ask what a professional must check before sending.

4. The course skeleton

Every course follows the same outline, so once you know one, you know them all.

  1. Welcome (2 min): who this is for, what you will be able to do, how long it takes.
  2. Modules (3 to 6): each module is a short intro, 2 to 4 lessons, then a knowledge check.
  3. Practical task: one real task the learner completes with AI, using their own firm's work.
  4. Final quiz: 8 to 10 questions, 80 percent to pass.
  5. Wrap up and resources: recap, plus downloadable prompt library, checklist and templates.
  6. Certificate and one click Add to LinkedIn.

Sample fully drafted lesson · 1.1 The five parts of a reliable prompt

Why this matters
The same question, asked two ways, gives two very different answers. In regulated work, "it depends how you ask" is a risk. A reliable prompt removes the guesswork.

What you will be able to do
Structure any prompt into five parts so the answer comes back consistent, defensible and in the format you need.

The idea, in plain English
A good prompt has five parts: role (who the model should act as), task (what to do), context (the facts and documents it needs), format (how you want the answer laid out), and constraints (the rules it must not break). Miss one and quality drops.

In your firm

Your input: one recurring question your team asks AI. For example summarising a new tax ruling, drafting a client explanation, or comparing two treatments. I will rebuild it live using the five parts.

Show me
A weak prompt: "explain Division 7A". A reliable one:

Role: You are a tax adviser writing for a small business client. Task: Explain in plain English what Division 7A means for a loan from their company to themselves. Context: The client is a sole director. Assume Australian tax law. Format: Four short paragraphs, no jargon, end with "what to do next". Constraints: If anything depends on facts you do not have, say so and list what you would need. Do not give a definitive figure.

The second prompt returns a client ready, appropriately cautious answer. The first returns a generic essay.

Your input: please sanity check the Division 7A example for accuracy, or swap it for a topic you would rather lead with. I want the flagship example in this course to be one you are completely happy to put your name to.

Try it
Take the weak prompt in the workbook and rewrite it using all five parts. Compare the two answers side by side.

Watch out
The constraints line is what keeps you safe. "If anything depends on facts you do not have, say so" is the single most valuable instruction in professional work, because it turns confident invention into an honest gap you can fill.

Takeaway
Role, task, context, format, constraints. Five parts, every time, and the answer becomes something you can defend.

Answer: C. Constraints turn invention into an honest, fillable gap.

Sample fully drafted lesson · 2.2 Deciding where a human must sign off

Why this matters
An agent that runs unsupervised in regulated work is a liability. An agent with the right review gates is an assistant. The difference is deciding, up front, where a human must approve before anything moves.

What you will be able to do
Look at any workflow and mark the points where a human sign off is mandatory, so accountability is never lost.

The idea, in plain English
Every automated workflow has steps. Some are safe to automate fully (gathering, drafting, sorting). Some must always pause for a human (anything sent to a client, filed with a regulator, or that moves money). You decide these gates before you build, not after.

In your firm

Your input: one repeatable task your firm would love to automate. For example, drafting a first pass BAS review, preparing a monthly client report, or triaging inbox queries. Give me the steps and I will map the review gates around your real process.

Show me
Take "prepare the monthly client report". The agent can gather the numbers, draft the commentary and format the report (safe to automate). It must then stop and wait: a human reviews the commentary and approves before it is sent (mandatory gate). Sending is the last step, and only a human releases it.

Try it
In the supplied worksheet, take a five step workflow and mark each step green (automate), amber (automate with a check), or red (human must approve). Justify each red.

Watch out

Your input: your professional rule for what must always be a red gate. My placeholder: anything that leaves the firm, is lodged with the ATO or a regulator, or affects a client's money or tax position, is always a human approval, no exceptions. Please put this in your own words, it is a key trust message for the course.

Takeaway
Decide the human sign off points before you build. Automation speeds the work up to the gate, a professional still opens it.

Answer: C. Drafting can be automated, releasing to a client cannot.
