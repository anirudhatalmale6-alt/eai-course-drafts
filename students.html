My honest read on the idea

Short answer: yes, do it, and I think it is the strongest new idea you have raised. But I would change one thing about how it is positioned and one thing about how it is sold, and both changes matter more than anything in the lesson list.

Why it is a good idea. Three reasons, in order of how much they are worth to you.

  1. It is a funnel into the business you already have. The students in a commerce or accounting society this year are graduates in a Big Four or mid-tier firm next year, and those firms are exactly who buys Courses 1 to 4. A certificate with your name on it sitting on a few hundred LinkedIn profiles is cheaper and better targeted than any advertising you could buy.
  2. It gives your sales team a reason to call a university. Careers services and student societies are hard to sell to and easy to give to. A free, genuinely good course is a door opener into an institution that also buys staff training.
  3. The demand is real and it is not being met well. Students are anxious about this and they are asking for it. What they are being offered is mostly generic.

Now the part I would not be doing my job if I left out.

The honest risk. This is the most crowded free-content market in education right now. Students in Australia already have free AI training available to them from several directions. Western Sydney University is fully funding up to a thousand of its own students through a Microsoft AI skills bootcamp this year. TAFE NSW, TAFE SA and the CSIRO national AI centre all run free introductory AI courses. Most universities give every enrolled student a LinkedIn Learning or Coursera licence through the library, and both are full of free AI content with certificates attached. If our course is a general introduction to AI, it is competing with all of that at a price of zero and losing, because they have distribution and we do not.

So the course cannot be an introduction to AI. It has to be the thing none of those free courses are, which is the reason I would draft it the way I have below: professional context, an assessed artifact, and academic integrity taken seriously. A student who finishes a Coursera AI course has a badge. A student who finishes ours should walk out with a piece of work they can put in front of an interviewer, and a defensible answer to the question they are now being asked in graduate interviews.

How it should be sold

This is the change I feel most strongly about. Do not sell it to students. Students are the worst customer in the market: no money, infinite free alternatives, and no ability to sign anything. Sell it to the people whose job it is to reach them, and let the students have it free.

The name, and the promise under it

The name is settled. What sits under it still needs one decision, and it is a factual one rather than a stylistic one.

The fear framing is "AI is going to take your job". It is the most motivating headline available and it is also the one most likely to be challenged, because the Australian graduate data does not currently support it. Graduate job postings in Australia stabilised in early 2026 and are sitting above pre-pandemic levels. What is actually happening is narrower and more useful: entry-level roles are being reshaped rather than removed, they are more competitive, and AI capability has moved from a differentiator to a baseline expectation. Employers are no longer impressed that you can use it. They assume it, and they are now testing whether you can use it with judgement.

That reframe is better for us in three ways. It is defensible if an academic or a careers adviser pushes back, which matters when you are an OpenAI Select Partner and your name is on it. It is just as urgent to an anxious student, because "the thing you are worried about is already assumed of you" lands harder than a prediction about the future. And it points at a course we can actually deliver, since we cannot teach anyone to be un-replaceable but we can absolutely teach them to work the way employers now expect.

The name

AI-Native Career Certificate

Build the practical AI capabilities expected in an AI-native workplace.

Your name, and it is better than any of the three I had drafted. The reason it is better is the word you changed, not the words you added. Calling it a certificate rather than a course moves it out of the category it would lose in. Nobody compares credentials the way they compare courses. A student choosing between our course and a free Coursera course asks which is cheaper. A student choosing between our credential and a free course asks which one an employer will recognise, and that is a question we can win.

It also changes who we are selling to without changing a word of the content. A careers service does not buy courses, it buys employability outcomes it can report on. A credential is reportable. A course is not.

Two notes on it, one that needs a small change and one that is just worth knowing.

The one thing we have to get right, and it is easy. In Australia the word "certificate" is not restricted, so we can absolutely use it. What is restricted is implying that non-accredited training is nationally recognised. Certificate I to IV and Diploma are protected AQF titles, and it is an offence to promote non-accredited training in a way that suggests it carries AQF status. So three rules for us: never put a roman numeral or a level after it, never use the word "qualification", and put one plain line on the certificate and the enrolment page saying it is a professional credential issued by Empathetic AI and not a nationally recognised qualification under the AQF. That line costs us nothing. It is actually an asset, because it is exactly the sentence that lets a university legal or compliance team say yes to distributing it.
Worth knowing, not worth acting on yet

"AI-native" is a current term and it is doing useful work right now, in the same way "digital native" did. Terms like that have a shelf life of a few years before they start to sound dated. That is not a reason to avoid it, because the next few years is exactly when this product needs to land, and by the time it ages you will have iterated anyway. I mention it only so it is a decision rather than an accident.

If you ever want the safer long-life version, the same positioning survives as AI Career Certificate with the AI-native language moved into the strapline where it is cheap to change. Not a recommendation, just the escape hatch.

Module 1 · What is actually changing about entry-level work

2 lessons · 26 minutes. Setting the record straight before teaching anything, so the rest of the course is believed.

1.1 What employers now assume you can already do

What you will be able to do

Describe the specific capabilities a graduate employer now takes for granted, and honestly assess which of them you have.

The idea

Two years ago, putting AI on a graduate application was a differentiator. It is not any more. The expectation has moved to everyday competence: knowing what the tools are good at, choosing the right one for a task, and applying judgement to what comes back. That last part is the whole ball game, and it is the part almost nobody is taught.

The lesson opens with a self-assessment against six things employers actually ask about, so the student knows which lessons they need most. It is short, it is honest, and it is the reason they keep going.

Do this

Rate yourself one to five on each of the six. Keep the sheet. You will rate yourself again at the end of the course and the difference is what goes on your application.

Takeaway

Nobody is impressed that you use AI. They are interested in whether you know when not to.

1.2 The tasks being reshaped, and the ones that are not

What you will be able to do

Explain, with examples from your own field, which parts of a junior role are changing and which are not, without either panicking or dismissing it.

The idea

The honest picture is more specific than the headlines. The first draft, the first pass of research, the first cut of a data set, the routine summary: these are being compressed. What is not being compressed is the part where somebody has to be accountable for the answer. Junior roles historically bought their learning by doing the compressed part. That is the real change, and it means the way you demonstrate value in your first year is different from the way your manager demonstrated it in theirs.

Worked through in three fields so it is not an accounting lecture: a graduate accountant, a marketing coordinator, a junior analyst.

Australian context. Graduate hiring in Australia has held up better than the commentary suggests, with postings stabilising in early 2026 above pre-pandemic levels. The pressure is on how competitive entry-level roles have become, not on whether they exist. We say this plainly, with the source, because a student who has been told the sky is falling and then finds out otherwise stops trusting everything else we said.

Knowledge check, end of Module 1. An employer asks at interview how you use AI in your work. Which answer is strongest?

A. "I use ChatGPT for everything, it saves me hours"
B. "I don't use it, I prefer to do my own work"
C. "I use it for first drafts and structuring data, I check every figure against the source, and here is a piece of work where I did that"

Answer: C. A sounds uncritical, B sounds unemployable in 2026, C is the only one that shows judgement and has evidence attached.

Module 2 · Using it without getting into trouble

2 lessons · 30 minutes. The module that makes universities willing to distribute this.

This module is the strategic centre of the course. Every free AI course aimed at students skips academic integrity because it is awkward. A careers service or a business school will not put its name near a course that teaches students to use AI without addressing it. Putting it second, before the practical work, is what turns this from something a university tolerates into something a university actively hands out.

2.1 Where the line is, and how to stay on the right side of it

What you will be able to do

Find and read your own institution's AI policy, and correctly classify a set of realistic scenarios as allowed, allowed with disclosure, or not allowed.

The idea

There is no single national rule, and this is the source of most of the trouble. Policy is set per institution and often per unit, and the same action can be fine in one subject and a breach in the next. So the skill is not memorising a rule, it is knowing how to check, and building the habit of checking before the assignment rather than after the allegation.

The lesson gives a decision path and eight scenarios to sort. Deliberately uncomfortable ones are included: using it to explain a concept you did not understand, using it to fix your grammar as a second-language speaker, using it to generate the structure and then writing the content yourself, using it to write code for an assignment that is assessed on the code.

Integrity note. The course never tells a student what their institution allows, because we do not know and it changes. It teaches them to find the policy, read the specific wording about generative AI, and ask the unit coordinator in writing when it is ambiguous. A written question before submission is a complete defence. A verbal assurance is not.
Takeaway

Check per unit, not per university, and check before you submit rather than after.

2.2 Keeping a record of what you actually did

What you will be able to do

Keep a lightweight process record that can substantiate your own work if it is ever questioned.

The idea

Detection tools produce false positives, and students who did nothing wrong get accused. The defence is not an argument, it is evidence: version history, notes, the prompts used, the drafts in between. This takes about two minutes per assignment and it is the single most practical thing in the course.

It also transfers directly to work, which is the point. Being able to show how you arrived at an answer is the same skill in an audit file, a client deliverable and an assignment. We say so explicitly, because it is one of the moments where a student realises the course is about their career rather than their marks.

Do this
For this assignment, keep one file with: - what you asked the tool, verbatim - what it gave back, unedited - what you changed and why - which sources you checked yourself Write it as you go. Reconstructing it later is not evidence.

Knowledge check, end of Module 2. You are accused of using AI on an assignment where you did not. What helps you most?

A. Insisting you did not use it
B. Running the text through a detector yourself to prove it
C. Your document version history, notes and drafts showing the work developing over time

Answer: C. Detectors are unreliable in both directions, so a detector result proves nothing. A visible trail of how the work developed is very hard to argue with.

Module 3 · Doing real work with it

3 lessons · 47 minutes. The practical core, taught on university work but written to transfer straight into a job.

3.1 Reading fast, and being able to cite what you read

What you will be able to do

Get reliable answers out of a long reading, a report or a case study without relying on a summary you cannot verify.

The idea

Same discipline as Course 2, pitched at a reading list rather than a lease. Do not ask for a summary. Ask narrow questions one at a time, require a quote and a page number for each, and require it to say NOT ADDRESSED rather than reason from general knowledge. Then open the source at the quoted page. The failure mode this catches is the one that gets students into real trouble: a confident, well-written, entirely invented citation.

Do this
Using only this document, answer each question separately. For each, quote the exact sentence you relied on and give the page number. If the document does not answer it, write NOT ADDRESSED. Do not use any outside knowledge.
The one that ends careers before they start. Fabricated references are the most common way students get caught, and they are the most damaging because they look deliberate. Every citation goes in your work only after you have opened it. No exceptions, no matter how real it looks.
Takeaway

Narrow questions, quoted sources, and you open the source yourself.

3.2 Turning a mess into something structured

What you will be able to do

Take unstructured material and get a clean, checkable table out of it, with the gaps visible rather than filled in.

The idea

This is the single most employable skill in the course, because it is what a lot of junior work actually is. Notes into a comparison table, a set of job ads into a requirements matrix, survey responses into themes, a pile of readings into a literature grid. The technique is the same one taught to professionals: specify the columns, forbid inference, and require MISSING where the source is silent. The value is entirely in that last instruction, because a table with no gaps in it is usually a table with invented cells.

Do this

Build a comparison table from four sources on the same topic. Then check three cells at random against the originals. Most students find at least one problem, and that discovery is the lesson.

Takeaway

A tidy output is not a correct one. Make the gaps visible and check a sample.

3.3 Writing that sounds like you, not like a model

What you will be able to do

Use AI in your writing process without producing text that reads as generated.

The idea

There is a recognisable register that these tools default to, and both markers and recruiters have learned to spot it. The fix is not a clever prompt, it is a change in where the tool sits in the process: use it to interrogate your draft rather than to produce it. Ask it what your argument is missing, where a claim is unsupported, which paragraph is doing no work. Then you write. The output is your writing, improved, rather than its writing, lightly edited.

Do this
Here is my draft. Do not rewrite it. Tell me: 1. what claim is unsupported 2. which paragraph could be deleted with no loss 3. what a sceptical reader would object to Then stop. I will do the rewriting.

Knowledge check, end of Module 3. Your AI-assisted literature table has a value in every cell across twelve sources. What should you suspect?

A. It did a thorough job
B. Some cells were inferred rather than found, because real sources usually do not all address the same points
C. The sources are unusually consistent

Answer: B. Completeness is a warning sign. Real material has gaps, and a table without any is usually a table that filled them for you.

Module 4 · Getting hired

2 lessons · 27 minutes. The reason they enrolled, placed where it pays off rather than where it hooks.

4.1 Your application, without the AI smell

What you will be able to do

Use AI properly in a job application, and avoid the specific patterns that get applications binned.

The idea

Graduate recruiters read thousands of these and they can spot a generated cover letter in a sentence. Worse, several large employers now treat an obviously generated application as a screening signal in itself. So the useful skills are narrow and specific: extracting the real requirements out of a job ad, checking your own experience against them honestly, preparing evidence for each, and pressure testing your draft. Not generating the letter.

Do this

Take a real graduate job ad. Have the tool pull out every stated and implied requirement into a table. Fill in your own evidence column yourself. The empty rows are your interview preparation and, more usefully, your plan for the next six months.

Do not have it write claims about you. Anything it invents, you will be asked about, and you will not be able to answer. Every line in your application has to be something you can be interrogated on for ten minutes.
Takeaway

Use it on the job ad, not on your own letter.

4.2 The AI question in the interview

What you will be able to do

Answer the AI question with a specific example and evidence, instead of an opinion.

The idea

The question is now close to standard in graduate interviews, in some form. Almost every candidate answers with an attitude, either enthusiasm or caution. The candidate who answers with a worked example and a verification habit is immediately in a different category, and this course has spent three hours making sure they have one to describe.

The structure taught: the task, the tool and why that one, what you did not trust it with, how you checked, and what you would do differently. Five sentences. Practised out loud.

I need you here. If you or Tristan have sat on the other side of graduate interviews and know what actually gets asked, one paragraph from either of you turns this lesson from good to unarguable. It is the lesson students will quote when they recommend the course.

Knowledge check, end of Module 4. Why does a specific worked example beat a strong opinion here?

A. Interviewers prefer detail
B. Everyone has an opinion, so an opinion does not distinguish you. An example with a verification step demonstrates judgement, which is what is actually being tested
C. It takes longer, showing enthusiasm

Answer: B.

Capstone and certificate

The capstone

One piece of real work, done with AI, submitted with a process note. It has to be their own material: an assignment they are doing anyway, a comparison table for a subject, an application for a role they actually want.

They submit two things. The artifact itself, and a process note of about two hundred words covering what they used, what they did not trust it with, how they verified it, and what they would do differently. The note is the assessed part.

The point is that they leave holding something. Not a badge, a piece of work with a defensible account of how it was made, which is exactly what module 4.2 taught them to describe in an interview. This is the difference between our course and the free ones, and it is worth the extra build.

Decision needed. Do you want the capstone reviewed by a human, or self-assessed against a rubric? Human review is far more valuable and creates a real relationship with the student, but it does not scale past a few hundred and someone has to do it. My recommendation is a rubric for everyone, with human review offered to society partners as the reason to pay for model B.

Certificate

Final quiz across all four modules, eighty percent to pass, same standard as the professional courses. Certificate issued on the quiz plus a submitted capstone.

The wording on it matters more than usual, because it will sit on LinkedIn profiles and it is our advertising. I would keep it factual and specific rather than grand. Something like:

has been awarded the AI-Native Career Certificate, a three hour applied credential covering professional use of AI tools, verification, academic integrity and workplace application, assessed by examination and submitted work.

Note "awarded" rather than "has completed", which is the difference between a credential and an attendance record. And note "assessed by examination and submitted work", the clause that separates it from every free completion badge and the reason a recruiter would give it a second of attention.

Footer line on the certificate itself, small but present: A professional credential issued by Empathetic AI. Not a nationally recognised qualification under the Australian Qualifications Framework.
